1. Lysis
▶

Buffer: Tris-HCl pH 7.4–8.0 + NaCl 150 mM + detergent + protease inhibitors (+ phosphatase inhibitors for phospho-targets). Keep cold/on ice — proteases are slowed.

The detergent dissolves membranes into micelles; SDS is very harsh: it disrupts membranes and unfolds proteins. Below the CMC a detergent works sluggishly.

Detergent mechanics: membranes are lipid bilayers; a detergent (an amphiphile: hydrophobic tail + hydrophilic head) inserts into the bilayer and dissolves it into micelles — proteins are released into solution.

The role of NaCl (150 mM) ▸

Salt creates a physiological ionic strength of the lysate. First, ions shield electrostatic interactions: without salt, proteins bind nonspecifically to each other, to membrane fragments and to nucleic acids, forming aggregates that are lost during precipitation. Second, NaCl competes for electrostatic contacts between DNA and histones of the released chromatin, lowering lysate viscosity (if the problem persists, add DNase I to the buffer). Third, osmolarity close to intracellular is maintained, which prevents osmotic shock of proteins. Practical consequences: the ionic strength of dilution buffers and calibration standards should match that of the samples; salt concentrations of 300–500 mM and higher dissociate weak protein–protein complexes.

2. Protease inhibitors: EDTA or cocktail
▶

EDTA covers only metalloproteases (chelates Zn²⁺ in the active site), cheap, single-component. A cocktail covers serine (AEBSF, aprotinin), cysteine (leupeptin), aspartyl (peps­tatin), and metalloproteases (EDTA).

Choice: protease-rich tissue (liver, pancreas, skin, macrophages), a long protocol, a sensitive target → cocktail. Routine work, robust target → EDTA is enough.

3. Protein concentration: BCA on an aliquot
▶

1. BEFORE denaturation — the measurement is done on a small portion of lysate (aliquot) while it contains no loading buffer, DTT, or heat.

2. Why exactly before: after electrophoresis preparation the sample contains SDS, a reducing agent (DTT/mercaptoethanol) and has been heated to 95 °C — all of this distorts BCA: DTT itself reduces Cu²⁺, giving a false signal, so measure before these additions. The bulk of the lysate is not consumed.

3. Bradford is incompatible with SDS (false background), BCA is incompatible with reducing agents (DTT). Standard BSA/IgG curve, reader at 562 nm.

Why Bradford is incompatible with SDS ▸

The Coomassie G-250 dye binds protein through attraction to cationic arginine and lysine residues and to hydrophobic patches. SDS coating the protein after lysis (~1.4 g per 1 g of protein) masks these binding sites — the signal is underestimated and loses linearity. At the same time, free SDS in the acidic Bradford reagent forms micelles and insoluble complexes with the dye: the turbidity gives a false background independent of protein amount. Two errors of opposite sign make the method unusable for SDS lysates. BCA chemistry (reduction of Cu²⁺→Cu⁺ and the BCA–Cu⁺ complex) is not disturbed by detergent — the method is compatible even with 5% SDS.

BCA assay protocol (microplate, 96 wells)
▶

Reagents: Reagent A (Na carbonate + Na bicarbonate + BCA + Na tartrate in alkaline solution), Reagent B (4% CuSO₄·5H₂O), BSA standard 2 mg/mL, flat-bottom clear 96-well plate. Equipment: reader with a 562 nm filter (540–590 acceptable), 37 °C incubator.

Calibration standards (dilutions from BSA 2 mg/mL):

  • A — 2000 µg/mL: 300 µL of stock
  • B — 1500: 125 µL buffer + 375 µL stock
  • C — 1000: 325 + 325 µL stock
  • D — 750: 175 + 175 µL from standard B
  • E — 500: 325 + 325 µL from standard C
  • F — 250: 325 + 325 µL from standard E
  • G — 125: 325 + 325 µL from standard F
  • H — 25: 400 µL buffer + 100 µL from standard G
  • I (blank) — 0: buffer only

Working solution (WR): Reagent A : Reagent B = 50:1, 200 µL per well (example: for 50 wells — 10 mL A + 200 µL B). On mixing the solution may turn cloudy; after shaking it is clear greenish; stable for one day.

Assay procedure:

  • 25 µL of each standard and sample into wells (duplicates/triplicates; dilute concentrated samples 2–10×)
  • Add 200 µL WR to each well
  • Shake 30 s on a plate shaker, cover the plate with a lid or film (evaporation)
  • Incubation: standard mode 30 min at 37 °C; Enhanced — 60 min at 60 °C (Pierce/Thermo — 30 min at 60 °C, G-Biosciences — 60 min; follow the specific kit manual; standards and samples must be incubated for exactly the same time)
  • Cool to room temperature (~5 min)
  • Read at 562 nm within ~10 minutes: the color keeps developing, delay distorts well-to-well comparison

Calculation: subtract the blank from all values → calibration plot (X — BSA concentration, µg/mL; Y — absorbance) → linear regression equation y = kx + b → substitute sample absorbances and multiply by the dilution factor.

Protocol variants: reagent-saving — 10 µL sample + 200 µL WR (range narrows to 125–2000 µg/mL); RT mode — 2 h at room temperature (the official Pierce variant); Enhanced — range 5–250 µg/mL; Micro BCA — 0.5–20 µg/mL, sample : WR = 1:1 (e.g., 150 + 150 µL), additional Reagent C, incubation 2 h at 37 °C or 1 h at 60 °C.

Choosing the standard for antibody-containing samples. BCA depends on amino-acid composition: IgG has fewer reducing residues (Trp, Tyr, Cys) than BSA, so a BSA calibration underestimates IgG-containing samples roughly 1.5–2-fold. For conjugates and immunoaffinity fractions calibrate with IgG or BGG (bovine gamma globulin — the gamma-globulin fraction of bovine serum, predominantly IgG; recommended by Thermo/Pierce for immunoglobulin-containing samples). IgG of different species are close in the composition of the determining residues, so BGG calibration is valid for IgG of any species; maximum accuracy — an IgG standard of the same species as the sample. Build the curve in the same buffer as the samples.

4. Equalization calculation
▶

Goal: the same µg of total protein in every lane (usually 10–50 µg) and the same volume. Samples of different concentration → different volumes → top up with make-up buffer (loading buffer page).

5. Denaturation
▶

95 °C, 5 min with loading buffer (SDS carrier + reducing agent). Full denaturation = heat + SDS + reducer: during lysis SDS does not unfold everything, and disulfide S–S bridges (covalent!) are not broken by SDS at all — only DTT/mercaptoethanol at heating breaks them. Without this a protein "ring" does not migrate according to chain length.

Sensitive membrane proteins: 70 °C/10 min (a compromise against aggregation during boiling).

Questions from our walkthrough

❓ Are proteins not already denatured during lysis?

Partially yes, but incompletely: domains are folded, disulfides intact. Heat reaches the core, DTT reduces S–S to 2×SH.

❓ What exactly does SDS do?

A harsh anionic detergent (C12 tail + sulfate): unfolds the protein and binds at ~1.4 g/g, imparting a uniform negative charge per unit mass → in the field proteins move by size only — the principle of SDS-PAGE. The flip side: it destroys conformational epitopes.

❓ Why is NaCl added to the lysis buffer?

NaCl (usually 150 mM) provides physiological ionic strength. Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions shield protein charges and prevent nonspecific aggregation with each other and with subcellular structures; the salt also competes for electrostatic DNA–histone binding, lowering lysate viscosity, and keeps osmolarity close to intracellular. Excess salt (300–500 mM and above) can disrupt weak protein–protein complexes, which matters when preserving them.

❓ Why does Bradford fail for SDS lysates while BCA works?

Coomassie in the Bradford reaction binds cationic Arg/Lys residues and hydrophobic pockets; the SDS "coat" masks them — the signal drops. Free SDS additionally causes turbidity (micelles and dye precipitates) — a false background. In BCA the defining reaction — reduction of Cu²⁺ by peptide bonds and Cys/Tyr/Trp followed by complexing with bicinchoninic acid — proceeds in the presence of detergent; the method tolerates up to 5% SDS.
